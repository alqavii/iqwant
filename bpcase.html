Case Study: The Suezmax Decision

An assessment-centre style trading case. Candidate reports describe a case pack (sometimes sent the day before), an analysis period, then a short presentation with questions. Read the brief, do the numbers, write a recommendation, present it in 5 minutes, then check the model answer.

45:00

The situation

You're a graduate analyst on the West Africa crude desk. The desk has bought a 1,000,000 bbl cargo of a light sweet West African grade ("Grade X"), loading in three weeks on a Suezmax. It was bought at Dated Brent + $1.20/bbl FOB.

The trader has three disposal options and wants your recommendation by the end of the session:

  1. Rotterdam: sell delivered to a European refiner.
  2. West coast India: sell delivered to an Indian refiner, a longer voyage at a higher price.
  3. Store: deliver to Rotterdam and put the cargo into tank for two months to capture contango, then sell.

Present your recommendation in five minutes: which option, the expected margin, the main risks and how you'd manage them. Assessors care more about your reasoning and how you handle uncertainty than about getting every decimal right.

All numbers are illustrative and built for the exercise. Ignore taxes and assume all prices are fixed as stated unless the question asks about sensitivity.

Other information

  • Flat price for financing: assume crude at $80/bbl; cost of capital 6% a year (simple interest, 365-day year), charged on days the cargo is at sea or in tank.
  • Indian refiners have been buying more Atlantic Basin crude after a cut in Middle East term supply. One broker report says two Indian refineries may enter maintenance next month.
  • Suezmax rates on long-haul routes have been volatile: a 10% move in a week is normal.
  • The European refiner is a long-standing customer of the desk.
  • Demurrage at Indian discharge ports has averaged 2–4 days recently; the Suezmax demurrage rate is $45,000/day.

Recommendation

On paper India wins by about $0.19/bbl ($190k). But that edge is thin relative to the risks: freight only needs to rise about 5.6% ($190k on $3.4m) to wipe it out, and three days' demurrage at $45k/day costs $135k ($0.135/bbl), cutting the India margin to about $0.30. A strong answer says this out loud.

A good recommendation: go for India only if freight can be fixed now (lock the Suezmax at $3.40m today) and the buyer confirms the cargo isn't affected by the reported refinery maintenance. If freight can't be fixed or the buyer is uncertain, take Rotterdam: a smaller but more certain margin, a shorter voyage, and it supports a long-standing customer relationship. Storage is clearly uneconomic: the $1.10 contango doesn't cover $0.80 storage plus about $0.80 extra financing.

Risks and hedges a strong candidate mentions

  • Flat price: the cargo is long oil from loading until pricing at the sale. Hedge by selling Brent futures or swaps and unwinding as the sale prices.
  • Dated timing (CFDs): purchase and sale price against Dated Brent on different dates; the gap can be hedged with Brent CFDs.
  • Freight: fix the ship now or hedge with freight forward agreements; it's the biggest swing factor for India.
  • Demurrage and operational: longer voyage, port congestion, quality claims on arrival.
  • Counterparty and credit: payment terms and letters of credit for a new or less-known buyer.
  • Commercial: the relationship value of the European customer is real but hard to price; mention it, don't overweight it.

What assessors look for

A clear recommendation in the first 30 seconds, numbers that are right or nearly right, a sensitivity that shows how fragile the answer is, and a "what would change my mind" line. Hedging the conclusion so much that you never pick an option scores badly.

Assessor follow-up questions

Practise answering these out loud in 60–90 seconds each.

  1. Your India margin depends on freight. What would you do in the next hour to reduce that risk?
  2. The trader says "Rotterdam is a sure thing, why bother with India?" Push back or agree?
  3. If the 2-month contango widened to $2.50, would you store? Show the maths.
  4. How does this cargo's price exposure change between loading and the day it's priced?
  5. What information would you most want that isn't in the pack?
  6. How would your answer change if Dated Brent fell $10 tomorrow?
  7. Which number in your analysis are you least confident about, and why?
Q3 hint: storage margin = Rotterdam 0.24 + contango − storage 0.80 − extra 61 days' financing ≈ 0.80. At $2.50 contango: 0.24 + 2.50 − 0.80 − 0.80 ≈ +1.14, which beats India. Contango needs to exceed about $1.80 before storage beats India.

Q6 hint: financing costs fall with flat price ($70 instead of $80), slightly improving every option, and longer voyages benefit most. Premiums against Dated are unchanged, so the ranking holds.
